# Gypsy Jazz Repertoire

Repertoire is the difference between a player who studies gypsy jazz and a player who can sit in. A modest list of tunes known cold — form, key, melody, comping, one chorus of solo — beats a large list half-learned.

## Choosing the next tune

Pick for a reason, and say the reason out loud.

- **Fills a gap:** no minor tune yet? No ballad? No waltz?
- **Teaches a device:** a tune that forces dim7 passing chords, or a tune in a key the student avoids.
- **Jam insurance:** a tune that gets called at every session in their area.
- **Ear training:** a tune with a melody worth transcribing.

Avoid learning two tunes in the same key and the same feel back to back.

## Learn-a-tune workflow

1. **Listen three times.** No instrument. Find the form: count the bars of the A section.
2. **Sing the melody** from memory, badly is fine. If you cannot sing it, you do not know it.
3. **Roots only** on the instrument, counting aloud. Mark the bar where the form turns.
4. **Guide tones** (3rds and 7ths), one per chord.
5. **Comp** the form with la pompe at a slow tempo, no melody.
6. **Melody** on the instrument, from memory, no chart.
7. **Solo** one chorus using only arpeggios, then one chorus freely.
8. **Play it at tempo with a recording**, and mark where it falls apart.

Only after step 8 is the tune "learned". Steps 1–4 can be done in a single session for an easy tune.

## The roadmap

For every tune, write a one-line roadmap before playing it:

```
Minor Swing — Am — 32 bars AABA — A: i–iv–i–V — B: iv→i→V — end on Am6, tag last 4.
All of Me     — C  — 32 bars      — C–E7–A7–D7 ... — end on C6/9.
```

Roadmaps are personal. They should be short enough to glance at between tunes.

## Keys

Keys in `references/tunes.md` are jam conventions, not laws. Singers and horn players move them. Before a performance:

- Ask what key, and write it on the set list.
- Learn each tune in at least two keys: the usual one and the one a singer will ask for.
- For minor tunes, know the relative major key too; someone will call it there.

## Intros and endings

Both are social acts. Agree them with a glance, or keep them boring and reliable.

- **Count-in:** four bars of la pompe. Nobody needs a drum fill.
- **Intro:** last four bars of the form, or a turnaround, or a pedal. Pick one.
- **Ending:** final chord on beat 1, 6/9 voicing, let it ring. Tags only if the band is watching.

`manouche-harmony/references/progressions.md` has the specifics.

## Jam readiness checklist

A tune is jam-ready when the player can do all of these without a chart:

- [ ] State the key and form in one sentence.
- [ ] Play the melody from memory.
- [ ] Comp a full chorus at tempo.
- [ ] Solo one chorus with a beginning and an ending.
- [ ] Recover from a dropped bar without stopping.
- [ ] End the tune the way the band expects.

If any box is unchecked, that is the next practice task, not a new tune.

## Set building

For a 45-minute set:

- Two burners to open, both familiar.
- One minor tune, one major.
- A ballad or waltz in the middle, when ears need a rest.
- One tune in an unusual key or feel.
- Close with the tune everyone knows.

Tempos should alternate. Three 240-bpm tunes in a row is a sprint, not a set.

## Teaching the repertoire

- Teach the melody before the changes. The changes are easier once the melody is in the ear.
- Teach the form physically: clap the A section, walk the bridge.
- Have the student call the form aloud while comping. It feels silly and works.
- Compare two recordings of the same tune: what changed, and why.

## Guardrails

- Never invent a tune's key, form, or composer. If uncertain, say what you are unsure about and point to the Roman numerals.
- Do not assign more than one new tune at a time.
- Do not confuse "can read it" with "knows it".
- If a student names a tune you cannot verify, ask them to hum it or describe the changes, and work from that.
